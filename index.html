“The future is already here. It's just not very evenly distributed.”
William Gibson

As a statistician, I take that literally. In medicine, the future is already strapped to millions of wrists. Almost none of it reaches the clinician making the decision.

The gap

Medicine sees you in moments. Your body lives in between.

The record

A few snapshots a year.

A hospital record captures a patient at the moments they seek care. It reveals little about the months in between.

The wrist

A continuous signal.

Wearables record how people sleep, move and recover, and how their physiology drifts from their own usual baseline.

The disconnect

It rarely reaches the clinic.

The challenge is not more data. A change can have many explanations, and both missed problems and false alarms carry a cost.

Why more alerts are not the answer

It's not what the model sees. It's how it guides.

11%

Of 264 patients evaluated after an Apple Watch flagged an abnormal pulse, only 11% received a clinically actionable cardiovascular diagnosis.

Wyatt et al., JAMIA 2020

92 vs 76

The team did worse than the AI alone. GPT‑4 on its own reached a median diagnostic reasoning score of 92%. Physicians working with GPT‑4 scored 76%, barely above the 74% of physicians without it.

Goh et al., JAMA Network Open 2024

What matters is what an assistant flags, when it steps in, and how it responds to the clinician's expertise.

My approach

AI that knows when to ask.

Whether it is an endoscopy frame, a recovery curve or a wearable signal, the same questions come up: what deserves attention, when to bring in a human, and how to show uncertainty so clinicians can push back. Here is that idea in one example.

01 · Baseline

Every patient is their own reference.

The assistant learns a personal baseline, so a change means "unusual for you", not "unusual for the average person".

02 · Change

Resting heart rate rises for six days.

It could be an infection. It could be harder training, or poor sleep. A naive system would fire an alert now.

03 · Ask first

The assistant asks for context.

Instead of alerting, it chooses a follow-up question for the patient. Adaptive questioning should separate real concerns from ordinary life.

04 · Involve

The clinician can push back.

When review is warranted, the assistant brings the evidence. The clinician can question it, ask for the raw measurements, or dismiss it. Dismissal isn't treated as proof the alert was wrong.

Questions I care about

From a good model to better care.

Most clinical AI never makes it into routine practice. These are the questions I want to work on to change that.

Timing

When should AI step in?

Which findings deserve a clinician's attention, and which only create more alerts.

Uncertainty

How should doubt be shown?

Communicating uncertainty so clinicians can question a result instead of simply accepting it.

Practice

Does it fit the workflow?

Designing with clinicians from the start, so tools fit how care is actually delivered.

Evaluation

Is the team better?

Measuring whether clinician and AI together make better decisions, not just whether the model is accurate.

A clinician agreeing with an AI is not proof the AI was right, and an accurate model is not proof it helps. Both need to be tested with the people who use it.

Wellbeing

Health starts long before the clinic.

Most of what keeps us well happens far away from a hospital: moving every day, eating good food, sleeping enough and spending time outside. It's the same everyday life that wearables capture, and the best clinical visit is the one you never need.

It's also where I recharge. I run marathons, lift and train calisthenics (the human flag is next), and I'm happiest outdoors, like on my trip through Australia.

Sunlight is part of it too. Daylight helps set our internal clock, which shapes how well we sleep, and an hour of golden evening sun on a porch is still my favourite way to end a day.
